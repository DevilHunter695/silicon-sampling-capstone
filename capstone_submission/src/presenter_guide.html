Group 55 Presenter Guide

What to say on every page of the demo, and how to give reasons during the quiz and the race

0. The story in 30 seconds

Say this first

We asked whether a language model can answer a survey the way an Indian respondent would. We gave Qwen2.5-7B a person's profile (sex, age, marital status, education, income, religion, caste, region, urban or rural) and asked real Pew questions. Prompted alone it scores 16.1%, worse than always guessing the most common answer (30.4%). After fine-tuning on respondent data it reaches 38.0%, with excellent calibration, but a gradient-boosting classifier on the same nine attributes still gets 40.0%. So fine-tuning fixes the model, but it does not beat the simple baseline.

1. How the model actually decides

This is the base for every reason you give. Keep it simple and do not over-claim.

  • One pass, no thinking out loud. The prompt (profile, question, numbered options) goes in. The model produces a probability for each answer digit (1, 2, 3, ...) at the next token. We normalise them and read them off. The prediction is the most likely option and its probability is the confidence.
  • What fine-tuning taught it. It saw 23,999 training respondents answering these questions. It learned, for each kind of person, which answers are common. In effect it learned a lookup: for people like this, the most common answer is X, and about this many chose it.
  • What it does not do. It does not reason about the person's life, values or beliefs, and we cannot see inside it. When you explain a prediction you are describing a measured pattern, not the model's thoughts.
The one idea that explains everything

For any group of similar people, a method can at best pick that group's most common answer, and it is right only as often as that answer is common inside the group. The fine-tuned model finds the right most-common answer for most groups (in the groups we checked, its pick matched the true most common answer in about four out of five), so its accuracy is capped by how concentrated people's answers are, not by how clever the model is. Opinions are spread out, so the cap is low, near 40% for every method.

The four methods in one line each

MethodOne-line reason for its behaviour
Majority classIgnores the person. Always answers the question's overall most common option. Accuracy is just how common that option is.
Gradient boostingLearns the same group lookup directly from the nine attributes, with no language model in the way. It is the ceiling for this information.
Fine-tuned QwenLearns the same lookup through the language model. It agrees with gradient boosting on 61% to 84% of answers (by question) and is about 2 points behind.
Zero-shot QwenHas never seen these surveys. It reads the profile, ignores real answer rates, and falls back on hedged, moderate answers (table in section 3).

3. Quiz: reasoning for every prediction

The four-step recipe, for any respondent and any question
  1. Start from the base rate. What is the most common answer to this question overall? (Majority column below.)
  2. Find the attribute that moves it. Each question has one or two attributes the model keys on (table). Look at that attribute for this person.
  3. Name the group's most common answer. That is what the model predicts. "People like this mostly answered X."
  4. Read the confidence. High means that group's answers are concentrated (children, unmarried). Around 30% to 45% means people in that group are split, so the model is picking the best of a spread. Explain a miss as this person is one of the group's minority, not as a model malfunction.
The quiz now explains itself

After every answer (and under every respondent card) a "Why the model said this" box appears. It follows the same steps as the recipe above: base rate, the attribute the model keys on, the group's most common answer, what the confidence means, the zero-shot habit, whether gradient boosting agreed, and a verdict that says how few people in that group gave the real answer. Read it aloud; it is built from the data, so it is always true for that respondent. Groups with fewer than 30 respondents are skipped.

Why the fine-tuned model never picks middle options

It predicts the single most common answer for the person's group. For opinion scales the middle options (such as "legal in most cases", "once or twice a month") are rarely anyone's most common answer, so they never win, even though real people choose them 15% to 20% of the time. This is a feature of picking the top option, not a bug in the model, and gradient boosting does the same.

"A base chat model has never seen these surveys. It hedges toward a polite moderate answer and ignores how Indian respondents actually answer. That is why prompting alone scores 16.1%. Fine-tuning replaces the hedge with the real answer rates."

The profile also matters to it, but in the wrong direction: on a 200-respondent subset the profile explains 29% of its prediction variance against 4% for real answers, and removing the profile raises its accuracy by 3.6 points.

If you get a quiz row wrong, or the model does

  • You wrong, model right: it applied the group lookup consistently. Say which attribute decided it.
  • Model wrong, you right: "this person is in the group's minority." Quote the confidence: at 40% it was always likely to miss one time in two.
  • Both wrong: the answer was unpredictable from the profile. That is the finding, not a failure of the demo.
  • Model wrong with high confidence: rare (about 13% of answers above 70%). Say so, and note the calibration table shows it is rare.

4. Race: what the lines mean

  • What it is. Random real test answers, scored one by one. Each line is a method's running accuracy.
  • Why the lines jump early. Few answers so far. Random error shrinks with the square root of the count.
Answers scoredRough error (one standard error) at 38% accuracyWhat you can conclude
30about 9 pointsNothing yet; the order can be anything.
100about 5 pointsZero-shot is clearly last; the rest overlap.

1,000about 1.5 pointsThe 2 point gap between gradient boosting and fine-tuned starts to show.
3,000about 0.9 pointsLines sit near 38.0, 40.0, 30.4 and 16.1.
  • Order to expect: gradient boosting, then fine-tuned, then majority, then zero-shot. Fine-tuned and gradient boosting are two points apart, so on a short race they may cross. Say "that is sampling noise, the full-fold numbers are 40.0 and 38.0."
  • Why zero-shot is last: its hedged middle answers are rarely the real answer (previous section).
  • Why fine-tuned beats majority: it knows which kind of person gives which answer, so it beats the question's overall mode by 7.6 points.
  • Single question races: the gap is largest on the children question (56% vs 32% majority). It nearly disappears on politicians in religion, because the profile does not decide that answer.
  • Axis: the chart now scales to the data. In the Full demo it runs to 60% for all questions (70% to 80% for a single question such as children). In the Selected demo it runs to 100%.
  • In the Selected demo: the fine-tuned line sits near 87% because the data is only confident answers. Say that outright, and that gradient boosting matches it there.

5. Questions you may be asked

QuestionAnswer
Is the model reasoning about the person?No. It is a single pass that outputs probabilities learned from training respondents. The reasons we give are measured patterns: which attributes its predictions change with, and what those groups usually answered.
Why only 38%?Opinions are spread out even within a demographic group. Every method using these nine attributes tops out near 40%. The meaningful comparison is the lift over the 30.4% majority guess, and the calibration.
So is the model useful?It is a calibrated, language-conditioned simulator close to a classifier. Its possible advantage, transfer to new questions, we could not confirm (28.9% vs 28.5% on 8 unseen items, p = 0.18, small and approximate test).
Why does it trail gradient boosting?Both use the same nine attributes. Boosting fits them directly. The language model adds nothing for these inputs and was fine-tuned for one epoch.
Why are the confident answers so good?They are nearly all the number-of-children question, mostly unmarried respondents predicted to have no children. A strong, simple rule that a classifier also finds.
Does this represent India fairly?Not yet established. Subgroup gaps exist (fine-tuned: religion 10.0 points, region 8.8, age 2.7). One fold and one seed; survey weights not applied.
What about the odd codes like [QRELSING] in questions?They are placeholders from Pew's questionnaire. [QRELSING] is the respondent's own religion; the model saw the code unfilled and got the religion from the profile. The demos show each code with its meaning. [OR] is a Pew codebook tag whose exact meaning we could not confirm; check the official questionnaire.
Is the demo live?No. It replays the stored outputs of the full run on all 46,209 answers, so you see exactly what the model produced.

Do not say
  • "The model understands Indian society" or "it reasons about the person". It does neither.
  • "87% accuracy". That is only for confident answers. Overall it is 38.0%.
  • "Fine-tuning beats classifiers". It does not: 38.0% against 40.0%.
  • "Zero-shot is below chance". It is below the majority guess (16.1% against 30.4%).
